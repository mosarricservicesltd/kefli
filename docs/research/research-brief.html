How read-it-later apps are actually built, and what a nostr version would borrow

A field survey of Pocket, Instapaper, Readwise Reader, Matter, Omnivore, Karakeep, Wallabag and Raindrop; the nostr clients that already touch this territory; and how listening and social features have fared. Ends with the decisions only you can make.

Revised September 2026: the protocol table in "The nostr layer" has been updated to match contract v1.1. The first edition proposed a NIP-37 wrapper for private saves and publicly shared audio blobs; both were dropped after the adversarial review (the first misuses a drafts spec, the second would publicly distribute copyrighted text and derived audio). The rest of this brief is unchanged market research. · Prepared for Richard · Sources are linked in the text and listed at the end · Some figures (Matter's price, Android availability) conflict between sources and are flagged where they appear

The short version

Every surviving app in this category has converged on the same skeleton, and users now treat it as table stakes: one-click save with a transient confirmation, a reverse-chronological library showing title, source and read time, a three-way state model of queue, archive and favourite, and a reader that strips the page and hides everything behind a single "Aa" control. The forks are in the organising metaphor (inbox triage versus folders versus a bookmark grid), pagination style, and business model. Social features were tried by Pocket, Instapaper and Matter and dropped or shrunk by all three; the one pattern that survived is a private-by-default save with an explicit second gesture to recommend. Listening has become expected, and the best implementations (Readwise Reader, Matter) put the play button inside the article, highlight word by word, and reconcile scrolling with narration.

On nostr, the pieces are all standardised and the closest thing to your idea already exists: Gigi's Boris, which reads your nostr bookmarks in a calm three-pane layout with layered highlights and, as of August 2026, a native Android build with TTS and offline reading. It stops short of the Pocket loop in two ways that matter for you: it surfaces relays, NIP numbers and zaps on its own landing page, and it has no iOS app, no share-sheet save of arbitrary URLs, and no private-by-default storage. Nobody has shipped the whole loop behind an interface that never shows an npub.

The stock pattern

Pocket set the template in 2007 and the others copied it. A toolbar click produced a "Saved to Pocket" popup with an inline tag field, a Remove link and a View List link (extension README); Karakeep and Wallabag's extension both keep the tag-at-capture idea, and Instapaper goes further the other way with a click that "sits quietly in your toolbar" and shows no dialog at all (TechHarry). Mobile saving is the native share sheet in every case. Readwise makes a point that its extension and Safari share sheet capture "underlying content rendered in your browser as opposed to just a URL", which is what lets it parse paywalled and JavaScript-heavy pages that a server-side fetch would miss (Readwise FAQ). That single design decision is the difference between the apps people praise for parsing and the ones they leave.

The library is a list. Pocket, Raindrop and Karakeep add thumbnails and offer a card or grid alternative; Instapaper and Reader stay text-dense. Pocket's own 2023 redesign, which pushed a Home tab of recommendations above the user's saves, was the most-cited reason people gave for leaving: one Hacker News commenter "finally gave up on it last year, mostly due to frustration with the terrible 2023 redesign of the mobile app" (HN). Another summed up the category's mood as "I don't want discovery. I want a personal box/shelf."

The reader is where the surviving apps spent their design budget. Pocket's 2018 refresh chose Blanco (a serif) for body text and Graphik for UI, arguing that since users spent about sixteen minutes a day reading, typeface selection deserved to be "a first-class interface component" (Pocket Design). Its published principles included "Calming so I can escape the noise of the Internet" and "Respectful of content" (Pocket Design). Readwise Reader defaults to 20px text at 1.4 line spacing, offers Atkinson Hyperlegible and OpenDyslexic, and chose vertical "paged scroll" over horizontal page-flipping because sideways flipping "disrupts the user experience" when highlighting across pages (Readwise docs). Progress in Reader is a thin gradient line at the top of the screen plus a current-paragraph marker. Every app exposes font, size and light/sepia/dark through one top-bar control and hides the rest.

Where they fork, and what the forks cost

The organisational metaphor is the real fault line. Reader's Inbox → Later → Archive with query-based Filtered Views suits high-volume readers and produces what one reviewer called "inbox pressure" (Marqly). Instapaper's folders-only model with Archive and Liked as the sole states is praised as simple and criticised as limiting (Marqly). Karakeep and Raindrop treat items as bookmarks in rich cards with AI tagging or nested collections, and lose reading affordances in the process: Raindrop has had an open request for offline reading "for 6 years now" (Raindrop feature request), and Karakeep users complain of tag proliferation from the LLM (HN). Matter tried a fourth model, a shuffleable Queue plus Subscriptions plus Discovery, after cutting its social tabs.

The second fork is trust. Omnivore was free, open source and widely loved for its parser, then ElevenLabs acqui-hired the team in October 2024 and gave users about a month before deletion; HN called the notice "insane" (HN). Pocket followed in 2025. The lesson users have drawn is that a sustainable price or self-hostability has become a feature in its own right, which is a direct argument for the nostr approach: the library lives with the user's key and outlives any single client.

Newcomers since Pocket's death (TechCrunch) are mostly recombinations: Folio, from Pocket's former head of product, reproduces the Pocket layout; Burn 451 deletes anything unread within 24 hours as a cure for the guilt backlog; Readeck is a self-hosted Go app with an iOS client. None of the sixteen apps TechCrunch listed uses nostr.

The nostr layer

Each feature you listed maps onto an existing event kind, and the mapping is worth fixing before any UI work because it determines what other clients can read.

Private savekind 30078 NIP-78 app data, one addressable event per URL, d = shelf/item/<hash>, content NIP-44 encrypted to the user's own key (NIP-78)
Where saves liveThe user's kind 10002 relays: ours plus two independent public relays by default, so the library outlives our server (NIP-65)
Parsed article textEncrypted blob on Blossom, mirrored to a second server with BUD-04; the user's kind 10063 lists both. NIP-96 is deprecated (Blossom)
Extension to phonekind 1059 NIP-59 gift wrap carrying only a hash and key; the page itself goes to Blossom so every event stays under 16 KB (NIP-59)
Better-voice audioCached on our own server, served only to signed-in users who saved the same article. Never a public blob or public index, for copyright reasons
Public recommendation (v2)kind 39701 NIP-B0 web bookmark, only on an explicit Recommend (NIP-B0)
Follow someone's recommendations (v2)Subscribe to their 39701 events; no new kind needed
Identity without jargonKey made on the phone, synced by iCloud Keychain or Google backup; standard NIP-06 recovery code; NIP-46 or NIP-55 for existing nostr users

One spec-level gap to design around: issue #2186 notes that an empty NIP-51 list gives a client no way to know whether new items should be encrypted or public. Your client should decide that for the user (private) and never rely on another client having done the right thing.

On hiding the protocol, Primal is the reference. Its Android onboarding takes a display name and an optional bio, auto-follows by interest, and never shows a key; the same review's main complaint was that returning users still had to paste an nsec to log in, which Primal has since fixed with QR "Remote Login" (nostr-reviews, humai). hodlbod's argument that key management is the main adoption blocker, and that NIP-46 should be the baseline with graduation to proper signers, is the sensible position (hodlbod). The contrast is YakiHonne, the most complete long-form suite on nostr across five platforms, whose reviewer warned that "terms like 'relays' or 'NIP-23' might confuse first-timers" (On Nostr), and Habla, whose Login button did not appear at all without a NIP-07 extension installed (Stacker News).

Boris deserves a proper look before you build, since it is the nearest neighbour. Gigi's 2023 essay laid out the exact blueprint: bookmarks and highlights as events, "swarm highlights" overlaying what readers found valuable, and "a special-purpose client [that] could aggregate bookmarked content into a distraction-free reading app, similar to Pocket," with TTS (Gigi). His August 2026 post says the web version "kinda worked, but also kinda sucked" as a daily driver, so he rebuilt it natively for Android; zaps, comments on highlights, friend-of-friend relay scopes, PDFs and books remain unbuilt (Gigi). Highlighter, which once occupied this space, has pivoted to creator subscriptions on NIP-29 groups (GitHub). Deepmarks is iterating a NIP-B0 social bookmark client with curator tiers (Nostr Compass). None of these has iOS, a share-sheet save of arbitrary web URLs, or private-by-default storage.

Listening

The placement question has a clear answer from the evidence. Readwise Reader puts a play button in the top-right of the document that starts "from your current scroll position", highlights word by word, and shows "return" and "jump" buttons when you scroll away so you can either snap back to the voice or move the voice to you (Readwise docs). Matter starts audio by long-pressing an item in the queue or from the article's menu, then shows a bottom banner that expands into a player with 30-second forward and 15-second back skips and skip-to-next-article; pressing both volume buttons highlights the sentence being spoken, so annotation works with the phone in a pocket (MacStories). Pocket framed its Listen as "your own personalized podcast" with continuous queue playback (TechCrunch). No surveyed app offers a paragraph skip. The New York Times built a separate Audio app on top of its Audm acquisition and then folded it back into the main app, on the reasoning that "the concept of there being an 'audio' audience is going away" (Nieman Lab); listening belongs inside the reading surface.

Sync highlighting is fragile in practice. Reader's changelog shows fixes for "word-by-word highlighting drifting out of sync" in August 2026 and for auto-scroll in February 2026, and a July 2026 note that first playback of an un-narrated document starts faster, which implies they generate once per document and cache (Readwise changelog). Word-level timestamps are the thing to get out of the TTS pipeline from day one.

On models, Kokoro-82M remains the cheap default: Apache 2.0, 54 voices, and fast on CPU, with one test paragraph rendering in 4.7 seconds on a twelve-year-old i7 (ariya.io); the Kokoro-FastAPI wrapper emits word-level caption timestamps and reports 35 to 100 times real time on GPU (GitHub). Chatterbox (MIT, about 0.5B parameters, roughly 4 GB VRAM) is the quality step up, with a listening test in which 65 percent preferred its Turbo voice over ElevenLabs (LocalAIMaster). Qwen3-TTS went Apache 2.0 in January 2026 (Simon Willison). Two popular models are unusable commercially: F5-TTS is CC-BY-NC and XTTS v2 is under Coqui's non-commercial licence. Every commercial reading app now meters audio (ElevenReader ten free hours a month, Speechify 150,000 words even on Premium), and the loudest complaints are about caps and dark-pattern trials (ElevenReader, CostBench). Nobody publicly documents a cross-user audio cache; the nearest are per-device caches and small self-hosted services keyed on text plus voice (tts-cache). Keying audio on article hash plus voice plus model version, and reusing the file for other users who saved the same article, is something no competitor advertises, and it's what makes a generous free tier affordable. The file stays on our own authenticated server and is never published openly.

Social

The history is consistent. Matter's first version had profiles, follows, likes, comments and activity notifications; Matter II removed all of it because "most users perceived them as a distraction from the core reading tool", keeping only follow-a-writer and send-to-a-friend's-queue (Matter). Pocket added Recommendations and follows in 2015 with an explicit privacy split, "Your saves to Pocket are (and always will remain) private. Only the stories you explicitly recommend are publicly available to your followers" (Android Police), and had quietly buried the feature by 2023. Instapaper's Likes-and-Friends feed kept interactions minimal to preserve its "passive, non-creepy nature" (MacStories). Readwise's founders describe themselves as "generally antisocial" about reading and ship only a per-document public link, off by default (Readwise).

The apps that inverted the default are small. Glasp makes highlights public by default and sells privacy back at $150 a year (MakerStack). Curius, "a shared library for your bookmarks", replaces the new-tab page with friends' recent saves and works well for a user who onboarded fifteen friends (Svitlana). Pinboard, "social bookmarking for introverts", has a Network tab of subscribed users' bookmarks, a count of how many others saved the same link, and a Privacy Lock; over half its users share nothing public at all (MacStories, Pinboard review). Mastodon keeps bookmarks fully private and the ActivityPub route to followable bookmarks is a separate server such as Postmarks (Andy Piper).

What the evidence supports: saves private by default, an explicit "recommend" gesture that publishes a single item, a follow feed of friends' recommendations on a secondary tab rather than the home screen, Pinboard's "N others saved this" count, and no likes, comments or notifications. Nostr makes this cheap because the private/public split is an encrypted NIP-78 save versus a bare public 39701, and following is a relay subscription.

The gap, stated plainly

A share-sheet save from any app on iOS and Android, readability parsing that captures rendered page content, private-by-default storage on relays the user doesn't have to know about, an offline reader with Pocket-grade typography, in-article listening with word sync and shared audio, and a quiet follow feed of explicit recommendations, all behind an interface that never shows an npub. Each piece exists somewhere. The combination does not, and the best-positioned builder (Gigi) has said in writing that the web version wasn't good enough to use daily and that iOS, zaps and social layers are still unbuilt.

Decisions I need from you

These are the things the research can't settle. Your answers become the brief that every later change gets checked against. Short answers are fine; "don't care, you pick" is also an answer, as long as it's explicit.

  1. Who is the first user, concretely?

    You alone for six months, your bitcoin circle, or Pocket refugees who've never heard of nostr? This decides whether jargon is tolerable at all and whether iOS ships in v1. My lean: Pocket refugees, which means zero jargon and iOS at launch.

  2. Where do you actually save from?

    Phone share sheet from X and newsletters, or desktop browser? The share-sheet flow gets designed first either way, but the answer sets whether the extension is a v1 deliverable or a v2. My lean: phone first.

  3. Queue model: inbox triage, plain list, or expiring?

    Reader's Inbox/Later/Archive, Instapaper's flat list with Archive, or Burn 451's forced expiry. This is the biggest single UX fork in the category. My lean: flat list plus Archive plus one favourite state, with tags optional and hidden until used.

  4. Do you want to read on desktop at all?

    If yes, the web reader is a v1 surface with keyboard shortcuts; if it's only a save target, it's a stub.

  5. Listening: how central?

    A feature on the article, or a podcast-style continuous queue you'd use in the car? My lean: both, with the in-article player first and continuous play from the Listen tab second.

  6. Which voice, and who pays?

    Kokoro on CPU is free-tier viable; Chatterbox on a GPU box is better and costs money. Shared audio makes either cheaper. Free forever, or a premium voice tier?

  7. Social scope for v1.

    None, follow-a-friend's-recommendations, or full public profiles? Every app that went further than "follow recommendations" retreated. My lean: recommendations and follows only, private default, no likes or comments.

  8. Highlights: in or out?

    Boris's three-layer highlights are the one nostr-native feature no centralised app can copy. They also add real UI complexity. In v1, or later?

  9. How much nostr do you want existing nostr users to see?

    A "sign in with your nostr app" button and nothing else, or an advanced screen with relays and key export? My lean: the button in v1, an advanced screen behind settings, never on the main path.

  10. Reader theme preference, from the four directions.

    Paper, Ink, Sage or Ocean, and whether sepia/dark are user options or a single fixed look. Your own reading habit answers this better than any survey.

  11. What's the one thing that would make you delete it?

    The failure story. Slow first load, a lost article, a redesign that moved your list, jargon on screen. Naming it now is what stops it creeping in later.

  12. Name and home.

    Under Based Department or standalone? The NIP-05 domain is visible identity for every user, so it needs deciding before onboarding is designed.
