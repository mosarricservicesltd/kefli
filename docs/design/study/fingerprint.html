Fingerprint

Every save draws its own cover from its address, identical on every phone for good, in an app that otherwise keeps to ink and paper.

01The concept

Every save already has a fingerprint: the sha256 of its normalised address, which is its name in the protocol (shelf/item/<sha256>, contract 4.3). Fingerprint draws that number.

Four bytes choose a set of four colours: one ground, one deep, one soft and one loud. Sixteen more seed a small grammar of quarter circles, discs, lenses and bands, laid on a square that splits into quarters and, here and there, into quarters again. The cover exists the moment a link is shared. It needs no network, no image server and no third-party picture, and it comes out the same on the phone, in the extension and on the website, for as long as the address exists.

That last part is why it suits Shelf in particular. The pitch says the library outlives the service; a restored shelf on a new phone comes back as the same mosaic, tile for tile, which makes the promise visible. Around the covers the app goes quiet: ink, paper and hairlines, no brand colour of its own, so the only colour on screen is the reading you chose. The chrome can be plain because the library is loud.

Influences, as influences

  1. Truchet tiles (1704)A few quarter-circle tiles, turned by rule, build patterns that look composed. The cover grammar takes the idea that a tiny vocabulary rotated by numbers can read as design.
  2. Key fingerprints drawn as picturesThe random art printed when an SSH key is made: a long number turned into something people can compare by eye. Here the comparison becomes recognition.
  3. Josef Albers’s colour studiesA colour is judged against its neighbours, so each cover’s four colours are built together as a set, with fixed roles in OKLCH.
  4. Patchwork quilt blocksA fixed set of shapes, endless variety, and a whole that reads as one textile. The Saves list and the You quilt are meant to read the same way.
  5. Library call numbersA short mark that finds the same book on any shelf in any branch. The cover is that mark, made visual.

  • 4 covers in lists and article images. 8 chips, segments, the tab tile and cover buttons. 12–14 buttons, cards, groups, players, the toast. 20 sheets and illustrations.
  • Everything in the flow is flat, separated by 1 px hairlines. Rows lose their cards and become a catalogue: the hairline under each row is also where its progress runs.
  • One float shadow, for things that hover over content (mini, Reader dock, Archive, toast). In dark it becomes a hairline ring.

Texture and imagery

  • Flat colour only. No gradients, grain, blur or transparency inside a cover, and never any text on one.
  • Every row has its cover from the moment of saving, offline included, and keeps it. A photo never replaces, overlays or recolours a cover.
  • Photos belong to the article. They appear only in the Reader, under the cover band, exactly as the author published them: never tinted or cropped into a tile.
  • A save whose text couldn’t be fetched keeps its shapes, drawn in the chrome’s greys at full strength, so the state looks chosen.

Icons · Lucide at 1.75 stroke, one colour

  • Stroke drops from 2 to 1.75 to sit with Archivo’s finer stems. Round caps stay.
  • Icons are text or muted, never coloured. The active tab sits in an inverted 8-radius tile.
  • In compact players the play glyph sits on a 30 px inverted disc laid on the article’s own cover: the button is the article’s face.
Signature visual move

Drawn once, then the same face everywhere

The moment a link is saved, from the share sheet, the extension or a restore, its cover draws itself: the square splits into quarters, the quarters split as the hash decides, and each shape lands in order over about half a second. From then on that face is the article wherever it appears: the row, the Reader’s header band, the play button in the mini player and the Reader dock, the Listen rows, the lock screen’s media art and the browser toast. Reading progress keeps its one line per article, and the line takes the article’s loudest ink that clears 3:1 on the surface under it, so the line and the cover belong together.

Photo or not, every article wears its cover on every surface, drawn whole and never mixed with a picture. An article’s own photos appear only inside the Reader, under the cover band, exactly as published.

Reduced motion: the cover appears already drawn. The drawing plays once per save, never on scroll.

What we lose when we stop reading long things

By Megan Garber · 11 min read

There is a particular kind of attention that only long reading asks for. It is slower than scrolling and less rewarding at first, and it builds something that skimming never does.

For most of the past decade, the tools we use to read have been built to keep us moving: one more headline, one more thread. The read-it-later apps were the exception, and their quiet decline tells us something about what we chose to value.

What they offered was modest. A list. A clean page. A promise that the thing you meant to read would still be there when you had the time to read it properly.

Extension save toast. The house inverted toast, unchanged in layout and copy. The success circle becomes the new save’s cover drawing itself in place, with a small green tick on its corner, so the first time you meet an article’s face is the moment you save it. The 2 px countdown runs in the article’s ink (chosen for 3:1 on the toast). The toast keeps system-ui, as the extension’s CSP requires.

States the cover already knows

05Honest notes

What is cool about it

  • Every save has a face from the first second, with no image fetch, no placeholder and no network. Offline, the library still looks complete.
  • The faces are permanent and portable. Restore on a new phone, open the extension, open the website: the same mosaic, which is the “outlives the service” claim made visible.
  • Articles without a picture (half the sample) stop looking second-class.
  • The chrome shrinks to ink, paper, two greys and hairlines: fewer tokens and fewer contrast traps than a brand palette.
  • Listening gains artwork: lock screen, media controls and car displays get a real cover for every article.
  • It is unusual. A read-it-later list that looks like a quilt is memorable without any illustration budget.

What could go wrong

  • Fatigue. A long list of loud squares can feel like confetti. The 2 × 2 base split, one loud ink per cover and ground-heavy weights calm it; a long-term test with a real 200-item library is the only proof.
  • Ugly outliers. Some hashes will produce muddy or garish sets. Render a thousand before freezing v1 and tighten the tables.
  • Forever is a promise. Changing the generator changes every cover people know. Version it (cover v1) and never edit v1; float drift between engines must be ruled out with precomputed palette tables and golden fixtures.
  • Meaning. A pattern says nothing about the article, and rows don’t show the article’s photo, so recognition rests on the title, the source and the cover becoming familiar. Titles must stay dominant. The photo is one tap away, as published, in the Reader.
  • Brand. Terracotta goes. Recognition moves to the icon, the type and the quilt. The icon is seeded by a placeholder name; renaming means choosing whether the seed changes.
  • Rule reversals. Soft pills said “no tile without a real image”; this gives every row a tile, shows the cover even where the article has a photo, and narrows imageless titles by 76 px (a 64 px tile and a 12 px gap). The Listen rows drop their thumbnail and the You quilt is new. All of these need decisions against the consistency spec.
  • Accessibility. Covers are decorative and hidden from screen readers; no information rides on cover colour. The progress ink falls back to the text colour when no ink clears 3:1. The drawing is the only motion and reduced motion removes it.

What it would take to build

  • Generator: about 150 lines of TypeScript in a shared package, used by the app, the extension and the website. Fixtures: 50 hashes with expected leaf lists and palette hex, run in every platform’s CI like the parser fixtures. 2 days.
  • Phone (Skia): each leaf is a Path or Circle in react-native-skia; a screen of covers is a few hundred paths. Render each cover once to an image, cache it by hash and size in memory and on disk. 2 days.
  • Photos: no image work in lists. The Reader shows the article’s own images as the parser extracts them, untouched. The grey cover for a failed fetch is the same path list with a fixed four-grey set per theme. An hour.
  • Drawing in: one Reanimated shared value driving each leaf’s scale and opacity, 0.5 s, off with reduced motion. Half a day.
  • Media art: write a 512 px PNG of the cover to the cache folder and pass its URI to the media session. Half a day.
  • Fonts: React Native can’t drive a width axis reliably on Android, so ship static cuts: Archivo Regular, Medium, SemiBold, Expanded Bold; Literata Regular, Italic, SemiBold; Martian Mono Medium. About 1 MB. Bundled locally in the extension (its CSP blocks remote fonts).
  • Icon: adaptive icon whose background layer is Shelf’s depth-1 cover (four shapes, a small vector drawable) and whose foreground is the bookmark and bar with the paper keyline; the monochrome layer is today’s bookmark and bar. 1 day.
  • Total: about a week and a half, most of it the generator, its fixtures and the cache. No server work and no contract change: the cover is derived from the d tag that already exists.
