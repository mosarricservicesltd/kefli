Paperback

A shelf of paperbacks: every save bound in the colour of its length, and every minute you read or listen inks its spine.

Shelf already calls itself a shelf, so Paperback takes it at its word. It borrows the confident economy of mid-century paperback publishing: flat ink on cream stock, a book face for the work and letter-spaced capitals for the publisher. Every save becomes a book with a spine, coloured by one rule, its length, so the Unread list reads from across the room as a stack of quick ones and long ones.

The unread part of a spine is printed as a halftone screen. Reading, or listening, turns it to solid ink, so progress becomes something you can see piling up, and Archive turns into a shelf of finished books. The chrome is loud print; the page you read on stays plain paper. That suits Shelf in particular: an app whose promise is that your library outlives the service should look like the most durable library object people know.

Influences

01
Colour-coded paperback lists

Inter-war and post-war series where one flat colour told you what kind of book you held, readable from across a room. Shelf takes the habit of a colour that means one thing.

02
Two-colour offset printing

A black plate over a single flat ink, with tints made from halftone dots. Cheap, confident, and the source of the halftone-to-solid progress.

03
Mid-century jacket typography

Geometric capitals, generously spaced, for the publisher; a book face for the title; wide margins of bare paper around both.

04
Library ephemera

Catalogue cards with a red head rule, spine labels, bookplates, the date stamp. The furniture of owning books, reused for the screens about owning a shelf.

02

The system

Paper, ink and one imprint colour do almost all the work. Three series inks appear only where an article is bound: its spine, its reading band, its cover. Nothing else on the screen is coloured.

Bound by length

The rule: under 8 minutes is Mustard, 8 to 12 is Rose, 13 and over is Petrol. A save with no text yet is a Proof, hatched in ink, and is bound when its text arrives.

Why length: it is the one fact about an article that changes what you do with it. A mustard spine fits a bus stop; a petrol one wants the sofa or your headphones. Colour by source needs a colour per website, hundreds of them, hashed into collisions that mean nothing. Colour by hash is pretty noise.

Why it stays calm: three inks at most; the longer the read, the deeper the ink, so the ladder still works in greyscale. Inks never sit behind words. Terracotta is the imprint and marks only Shelf’s own actions.

Three reading palettes

Body text 18 px Literata at 1.6, at least 7:1 in each. The band under the top bar is the article’s ink; the spoken word takes a tint of it.

Type

Two families, both OFL on Google Fonts. Literata for the work (titles and reading), Jost for the publisher (sources, labels, controls, numbers). No mono.

Shape, texture, imagery, icons

A printed look: flat ink and keylines, with no shadows, gradients or pills.
Shape language

Two shapes. Rectangles with a 2 px corner for everything you read or press (cards, fields, buttons, segments, sheets, the player). Roundels only for things that play. Two line weights: a 1.5 px ink keyline on anything interactive (3:1 or better in both themes) and a 1 px hairline between rows. Elevation comes from a keyline alone. The Terracotta gradient retires: imprint is one flat ink.

Texture and imagery

Halftone: a 45° screen at 8 px pitch, dots 1.8 px, over a 16% tint of the ink (6 px for spines under 12 px wide). Unread is screen, read is solid. Covers: the lead image becomes a black plate (greyscale, contrast up) printed over the article’s ink, in lists and on Now playing only. Inside the Reader, images appear as published. No paper grain: on a phone, grain reads as dirt.

Icons

Lucide, 2 px, in ink. The active tab’s icon sits in a Terracotta label, white. Play and pause are filled. Icons never take a series ink, and status icons keep their meaning colour. The one custom glyph is Speed read’s frame, already drawn for the prototype.

Signature move

The line that already carries progress becomes the article’s binding.

Reading inks the spine

Every article is bound in one band of its series ink, and the band travels with it: the spine on its row, the band under the Reader’s top bar, the top edge of the player, the cover of Now playing, the bar in Speed read and the strip on the extension’s toast. The part you haven’t reached is a halftone screen; whatever you have read or heard is printed solid. One stored position drives every band, so ten minutes listened to in the car show up on the shelf.

A save arrives as a hatched Proof and is bound in its ink the moment its text lands, a 250 ms wipe from the top (opacity only with reduced motion). Over weeks, Archive fills with solid spines: the books you finished.

03

On the phone

Real size, 360 × 760, drawn in HTML and CSS with the prototype’s copy, articles and controls. Only the visual identity has changed.

What changes in the dark

Paper becomes night stock (#17150F), a warm black, so the inks keep their print character.

Inks lift one step (Mustard #E2B04F, Rose #E8909E, Petrol #5FA6AE) and keep their light-to-deep order. Because inks never carry words, they can change per theme without breaking any text pair.

Keylines soften to #8C8372 (4.9:1 on night stock): still a clear boundary, without a lattice of bright lines.

Terracotta does not move. #B5542E with white content in every theme and reading palette, as decision 61 already says.

What we lose when we stop reading long things

By Megan Garber

Somewhere between the third tab and the fourth notification, a long article turns into a promise. You will get to it later: on the train, at the weekend, when things calm down.

The trouble is that later rarely arrives with the patience of the moment you found the piece. Long reading asks for an hour that has to be protected, and the web is very good at taking hours apart.

So we keep things instead. Tabs, bookmarks, screenshots, a folder called “read”.

The save toast

The house inverted toast, in ink on a light page. Its left edge is the new save’s spine: the extension parses the article, so it already knows the length and binds it in Rose (11 minutes) as the toast arrives.

Same anatomy as today: success roundel, “Saved to Shelf”, the page title, Undo, ✕ and the countdown, now a 3 px Terracotta band.

On a dark page the toast flips to paper, and the strip uses the dark inks.

Build: plain CSS in the closed shadow root. Halftone is two radial gradients. The toast can stay in system-ui, as the extension spec asks, because the spine carries the identity.

05

Honest notes

What makes it cool, what could go wrong, and what it would take.

What is cool

  • The list has a picture. Before reading a word you can see the mix (two quick ones, a long one) and how far through you are.
  • Progress becomes a collection. Archive gets a payoff: a row of solid spines, the books you finished.
  • It is ownable. No read-it-later app looks like a bookshop. The spine works as an app icon, a website motif and a 10 px strip on a toast.
  • Boring screens get real objects from the same world: an empty shelf, a bookplate for the backup, a catalogue card for settings, a date stamp for the warning.
  • The colour carries meaning, and it is already in the data: the meta line says “9 min”, the spine says it at a glance.

What could go wrong

  • Fatigue. Three inks on every row could tip into a children’s library. Mitigation: colour lives only in a 28 px spine and never behind words; test with a 40-item list before committing.
  • The rule is invisible at first. People may read colour as source or topic. The meta line repeats the length, so it is learnable in a day; a one-line legend on first run would need to go on the flow map first.
  • Kitsch. Bookplate, catalogue card and stamp are each used once. More than that (wood, faux paper, page curls) turns it into a theme park.
  • Moiré. An 8 px dot screen can shimmer while scrolling at some densities. Snap the pitch to whole device pixels and check on the spike phone.
  • Accessibility. Colour is never the only cue, inks never carry text, keylines give 3:1 edges. The stamp is rotated 2.5°, which costs a little for low vision; it stays 13 px bold and reads normally to screen readers.
  • Brand change. Pills, the gradient and soft shadows all go. Every component in the prototype changes shape, and the icon is new.

What it would take

  • Tokens: add series inks per theme, the length thresholds (in shared defaults, so the extension binds the same way), halftone pitch and dot, radius 2; remove the gradient and shadows.
  • Spines and bands (React Native): a View with a tiled halftone (a 12 px PNG tile with resizeMode repeat, or a react-native-svg Pattern) under a solid View whose height follows the stored position, animated with Reanimated. No Skia needed.
  • Covers: RN 0.77+ on the New Architecture has filter and mixBlendMode on Android; check them on the spike phone. Fallback: react-native-skia (a ColorMatrix and multiply over a fill) baking each cover once at save time into a cached JPEG, so lists scroll plain images. Skia adds several MB per ABI.
  • Fonts: Jost and Literata static weights through expo-font, Latin subset. Literata is the heavier family; ship 400, 400 italic, 600, 700, 800 and nothing else.
  • Extension and website: plain CSS; halftone and hatch are gradients. Fonts bundled locally in the extension (CSP).
  • Effort: about a week to re-skin the prototype and tokens, a day for the icon set, then the spike check on covers and moiré.
