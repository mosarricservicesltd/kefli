01 · The conceptRiso direction

Shelf printed like a two-colour zine: fluoro pink and riso blue on newsprint, loud on the covers and calm on the page.

A risograph prints one ink at a time, and its inks are translucent, so wherever two of them cross, a third colour appears for free. Overprint builds Shelf from that rule. Blue prints the line work, pink prints the fills, yellow prints only what is being read aloud, and a midnight key ink prints the words. The chrome gets the full print-shop treatment: chunky expanded type in two plates slightly out of register, every photo turned into halftone dots, states stamped on like a library’s date-due slip. Inside, the Reader drops to one ink on one stock, and the only thing that moves is a yellow highlighter on the spoken word.

It suits Shelf because Shelf’s promise is a library you keep, and print is the oldest format that outlives the press that made it. A zine costs almost nothing, belongs to whoever holds it and gets passed around for decades. That is the feeling a free, account-free library should have, and it lets a deliberately plain app (a list, a page, a voice) look like something a person made by hand.

Shape language

Die-cut corners, no shadows

  • Radius 3 on every rectangle: cards, fields, buttons, chips, the player. Circles only for play, the floating Archive and dots. Sheets keep 12 on their top corners.
  • No shadows. Ink doesn’t cast them. Elevation is a change of stock: bright stock for fields, sheets and the tab bar, a 14% pink tint for cards.
  • Lines are blue, 2 px for controls and 1 px for ruled lines. Fills are pink. Content on any fluoro fill (pink, yellow, flame) is midnight ink in every theme and palette. Blue fills only the swipe reveal, and what sits on it prints in the paper colour.
  • Out of register by 3 px on the screen’s one primary and the full-size play button only. Pressing slides the blue line into register, like a stamp coming down.
Texture and imagery

Every photo is printed

  • No photo appears in full colour. Lists print it in one ink (blue, 4 px screen). The Reader prints two plates, blue and pink, 1.5 px out of register on a 3 px screen. On black stock the plates are made from the negative, so ink lands on the highlights and the picture reads the right way round.
  • Grain is for ink areas of 24 px and up: fills, stamps and display type. Never behind text below 20 px, and never on the Reader page.
  • Articles without an image get no tile, as today. There are no placeholder halftones.
Icon treatment

Lucide, printed in the line plate

  • Lucide at 2.25 px stroke, a touch heavier so it reads as printed, plus Shelf’s own skip 15 pair and Speed read frame.
  • Icons print blue or midnight, never fluoro on paper. The selected tab gets a pink ink blot behind a midnight icon, a little irregular, as if inked by hand.
  • States become stamps: a short word in a double rule, set in the mono at a slight angle. They sit beside the real copy and are hidden from screen readers.
The signature move

Solid ink is where you’ve been

Every progress mark in Shelf is a row of halftone dots that fills in to solid blue as you read or listen. It runs along the bottom edge of a part-read card, under the Reader’s top bar, along the player and across Speed read, all drawn from the one stored position. Dots are still to come and solid is done, so the strip reads at a glance without a number, and it is the same idea as the thumbnails: tone is made of dots, and reading fills them in. On the yellow player the blue prints over yellow and turns olive, which is the overprint rule doing its job.

Extension · save toast on a white article page

Stamped as saved

The house inverted toast becomes a midnight slip with a pink dot for the tick and a SAVED stamp that lands on its corner in 260 ms (a fade with reduced motion). Copy, Undo, ✕ and the eight-second countdown, now a pink bar, are unchanged. The toast keeps its shadow-root contract and still never takes focus.

05

Honest notes

What earns the noise, what could go wrong, and what it would cost to build.

CoolWhat is good about it

  • It is unmistakable. A pink tile on the home screen and a page of halftone dots belong to nobody else among reading apps, and both survive being shrunk to a thumbnail in a store listing.
  • The rules are physical, so they are easy to keep. Lines are blue, fills are pink, the voice is yellow, words are midnight. A new screen nearly designs itself, and a wrong colour looks wrong at once.
  • Variety for free. Violet, flame and olive only appear where two inks cross, so the palette feels richer than four colours without anyone choosing a fifth. They are precomputed tokens, not effects.
  • Progress gets a texture. The strip says “still to come” with dots and “done” with ink, on every surface, from one position.
  • The noise stops at the Reader. One ink on one stock, Newsreader at 18/1.6 and at least 11.8:1 in every palette, so the twenty-minute read is untouched.

RiskWhat could go wrong

  • Fatigue. Fluoro ink every day can wear thin. Keep it under a sixth of the screens people live in (measured on these boards: Saves 9%, the Reader 7%, You 5%, first run 7%) and let blue do most of the work. Listen is the exception at 26%, because its Now playing card is the voice plate’s one big moment; if that tires, the card can drop to a yellow band.
  • Contrast traps. Fluoro pink is 2.7:1 on paper and yellow 1.1:1, so neither may carry information alone; pink text uses dense pink (5.5:1), and the words inside a stamp print clean, with only its rules worn. Misregistered type can look like blur or double vision to some people, so the plates register (offset 0) whenever the phone’s high-contrast text or remove-animations setting is on.
  • Halftone loses detail. At 72 px faces, charts and screenshots become dots. Fine for recognising a save, less fine for a chart-heavy article; the Reader’s finer two-plate screen helps, and a long-press could show the original.
  • It could read as a trend. Riso was everywhere in indie design in the late 2010s, and pink with blue is its most common pair. The yellow voice plate and the stamp system are the parts that belong to Shelf, so they should be the last things cut.
  • It resets the brand. Terracotta, the gradient, Soft pills and the “no blue or purple” rule all go, and the app icon starts again.
  • Screens can’t fluoresce. sRGB pink is a stand-in for real fluoro ink. Display P3 values could push saturation on phones that support it.

BuildWhat it would take

  • Tokens. A riso style in tokens.json: four inks per theme, three overprints, three reader palettes. Overprints are precomputed hex values, so most screens need no blending at all.
  • Fonts. Static instances of Archivo (Expanded Black, SemiExpanded ExtraBold, SemiBold, Medium), Newsreader (Regular, Italic, SemiBold) and Martian Mono (Medium and Bold at two widths): about 1 MB of TTF through expo-font. The extension bundles them as local @font-face (its CSP forbids remote fonts); the website can use Google Fonts.
  • Halftone. Either one react-native-skia runtime shader that samples each 4 px cell and draws a dot (Skia adds roughly 4 to 6 MB to the Android download), or a one-off pass in the hidden WebView’s canvas when a save is fetched, cached as a small two-colour PNG, with no new native dependency. The extension and website use the SVG filter on this board.
  • Grain. One 256 px noise PNG used as a mask, through Skia or a masked view. Nothing generated at run time.
  • Misregistered type. Two Text layers 2.5 px apart. The violet overlap needs mixBlendMode (React Native 0.77 and later, New Architecture, which Expo uses by default) or Skia text with a multiply blend; without either, fall back to the overlap colour alone.
  • Stamps and motion. react-native-svg with the grain mask; the landing is a 260 ms Reanimated scale and rotation, opacity only with reduced motion. Press into register is a 3 px translate on press-in.
  • Spoken-word highlight. A skewed yellow rectangle behind the word needs the word’s box from onTextLayout, or a Skia paragraph.
  • Effort. Roughly one to two weeks over the planned component work, most of it the halftone pipeline and testing blends on cheap Android phones.
