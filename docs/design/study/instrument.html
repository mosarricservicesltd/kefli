Instrument

Shelf as a well-made machine for reading: warm-grey faceplate, one signal orange, numbers lit in little windows, and a page that stays paper.

Read-it-later is a utility, so this direction dresses Shelf as the best kind of utility, an object you would keep for twenty years. Every screen is treated as a faceplate. The chrome is warm grey and off-white, hairlines and legends, and the only colour is a signal orange that means one thing: live, or yours to act on. Numbers a machine produces (clock time, counts) are lit in recessed segment windows; human estimates such as “4 min left” stay in words.

Listening is where it comes alive. Now playing becomes a deck with a tuning dial numbered in minutes, a time readout, a word meter that ticks with the voice, and a transport bank of keys that travel. Then you open an article and the machine steps back: flat paper, a reading serif, nothing else. It suits Shelf in particular because Shelf’s promise is durability. A library that outlives the service should look like something built to last.

The system

Faceplate, signal, paper

Two themes and one signal. The greys are warm and sit close together, so hierarchy comes from tone, hairlines and type rather than shadows. Signal orange #E04E14 is a physical part: the same value in light, dark and every reading palette. It marks four things only (the active control, the play key, lit progress and lamps) and it never letters text on a light surface.

Three reading palettes

The Reader is the one place the instrument switches off: flat paper, no grain, no grid, no lamps. The palettes are named here for what they look like; the Aa sheet keeps its labels Light, Sepia and Dark. Literata 18/1.62 on all three, every body pair above 7:1.

The quiet return of the read-it-later app, and why it matters this time

The first time a page disappears from the web while it is still on your list, you learn what a read-it-later app is actually for. It was never about saving time. It was about keeping the thing itself, the words, somewhere that belongs to you.

NumeralsDrawn, not typed: a seven-segment component with the unlit segments visible. Clock time and counts only. Screen readers get the plain number.
Words for human estimates: 4 min left, 9 min. Segments for what a clock or a counter would show.

Shape language

Radius 6 for modules and keys, 3 for windows and photos, 16 for sheet tops, round only for lamps. Content panels have no soft shadow: tone plus a hairline. Keys carry a 2 px travel edge (3 px on Signal) and drop 1 px when pressed. Only floating objects cast a shadow (mini player, Reader dock, toast).

Texture and imagery

Bead-blast grain at about 4% on the chassis, never on the page. Corner ticks on content panels. One speaker grille, on Now playing. A hairline dot grid for illustrations. List thumbnails are warm monochrome; the article that is playing shows in colour, and the Reader’s figure is always in colour.

Icons

Lucide geometry re-cut for a faceplate: 1.75 stroke, square caps, mitred joins (dashed row: today’s 2 px round). 20 px in rows and keys, 24 px on the transport. Icons sit on keys or lead rows; they never float as decoration. Shelf’s own skip and Speed read glyphs take the same cut.

Signature move · the needle

Every article has one graduated scale and one needle. On a card it is a hairline ruler along the foot of the module, the read part lit orange and an ink needle at your place. The Reader dock and the mini player carry the same scale, smaller. On Now playing it grows into a tuning dial numbered in minutes, and the needle moves as the voice reads. It is one stored position drawn one way everywhere, so the eye learns it once; and because the needle is a hairline indicator with no knob, it never invites dragging (there is no seeking in v1).

Honest notes

What it gets right, what could go wrong

What is cool about it

  • One colour with one meaning. Orange always says live or act here, so a glance at any screen finds the play key, the active list and your place.
  • The boring screens get hardware. Counts sit in readouts, states are lamps, a value has its own window, and Delete my data carries a guard stripe. The You tab looks like the back panel of something good.
  • Listening has a body. Dial, readout, word meter and a transport bank make the TTS feel like an object you operate, which is where a free app can feel more crafted than a paid one.
  • The Reader stays paper. All the character lives in the chrome; the page is Literata on flat paper at 12 to 16:1.
  • It ages well. Warm greys, hairlines and one orange date far more slowly than gradients and glass.

What could go wrong

  • Costume creep. Keys, lamps and grilles can tip into skeuomorphic fancy dress. Limits: one grille, lamps only for real states, travel edges of 2 px at most, no screws, no brushed metal.
  • Fatigue. Warm grey for years can feel drab. The orange and the richer dark theme carry it; test a week of daily use before committing.
  • Segment numerals. They are less familiar than type and near the legibility floor at 12 to 14 px in the mini and dock. Each readout has a plain-text label for screen readers, and with large or bold text settings the app should swap segments for Archivo tabular figures.
  • Capital legends. Condensed capitals at 13 px read slower than mixed case, so they are held to one or two words, never sentences.
  • Signal against error. The orange and the error red are neighbours. Error is darker, and always comes with an icon and a word.
  • Contrast margin. Ink on Signal is 4.77:1 and Signal on the chassis is 3.12:1: both pass, neither by much. Any tint change needs remeasuring.
  • New visible words. The transport legends (Back, Pause, Forward, Speed) restate existing accessible names, but they are new on screen and need adding to the flow map first.
  • Resemblance. Segment numerals with orange on grey sit near some audio-hardware and phone brands. Keep the segments inside windows, keep the greys warm, and never set words in segments.

What it would take to build

  • Tokens. Same shape as tokens.json with new values, plus roles for raised, well, tick, window, ghost and signal-edge. Roughly a day including the generator.
  • Fonts. Archivo and Literata, both OFL. React Native cannot drive a width axis on Android, so ship static instances (Archivo Condensed SemiBold, Regular, SemiBold, SemiExpanded Bold; Literata Regular, Italic, SemiBold), about 700 KB. The extension bundles the variable files locally (its CSP forbids remote fonts); the website loads Google Fonts.
  • Segment numerals. A small react-native-svg component (seven polygons per digit) and the same markup as inline SVG on the web. No Skia. One to two days with the accessibility labels.
  • Scale and needle. Plain Views with the needle moved by Reanimated on the UI thread. Cheap.
  • Word meter. A Reanimated value bumped on each expo-speech onBoundary event. It depends on the spike: if onBoundary has no positions the meter stays dark rather than faked.
  • Keys. Pressable with a bottom-edge View, translateY 1 on press and a light haptic (expo-haptics). The lamp glow uses RN 0.76 boxShadow, or a tiny radial PNG.
  • Monochrome thumbnails. Skia ColorMatrix, baked once at save time and cached, since a live Skia canvas per row is heavy in a long list. About a day. Grain and grille are tiled PNGs, no Skia.
  • Total. About eight to ten working days on top of the planned UI build, most of it in the deck and the segment component.

Measured contrast

WCAG 2.1 relative luminance, computed from the hex values on this board. Body text in every reading palette clears 7:1; every text pair clears 4.5:1; lines, lamps and icons clear 3:1.
